From kinematics to kinetics

Chapter 1: kinematics

  • Position, velocity and acceleration of every link and point.
  • ω\omegaω and α\alphaα of each link; piston velocity and acceleration.
  • No forces considered, only the geometry of motion.

This chapter: kinetics

  • The forces and torques that produce that motion.
  • Inertia forces from the accelerations we already found.
  • The driving torque needed, and how the flywheel smooths it.

You cannot do the inertia-force analysis without the accelerations from Chapter 1. That is why kinematics came first.

Newton's laws of motion

The kinetics of machine parts rests on Newton's three laws. For a rigid body of mass mmm and mass moment of inertia IGI_GIG​ about its centre of mass G:

∑F=m aG\sum F = m\,a_G∑F=maG​translation of the centre of mass
∑MG=IG α\sum M_G = I_G\,\alpha∑MG​=IG​αrotation about the centre of mass
  • First law: a body stays at rest or in uniform motion unless a net force acts on it.
  • Second law: the rate of change of linear momentum is proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of that force.
  • Third law: the forces at a joint are equal and opposite on the two links. This is what makes free-body diagrams work.

In the plane this gives three scalar equations per rigid body: ∑Fx=maGx\sum F_x = m a_{Gx}∑Fx​=maGx​, ∑Fy=maGy\sum F_y = m a_{Gy}∑Fy​=maGy​, ∑MG=IGα\sum M_G = I_G \alpha∑MG​=IG​α.

D'Alembert's principle

The inertia forces and couples, together with the external forces and torques on a body, put it in statical equilibrium.

Fi=−m aGF_i = -m\,a_GFi​=−maG​inertia force: through G, opposite to the acceleration
Ti=−IG αT_i = -I_G\,\alphaTi​=−IG​αinertia torque: opposite to the angular acceleration
  • Adding them lets us treat a dynamics problem with the tools of statics: ∑F=0\sum F = 0∑F=0, ∑M=0\sum M = 0∑M=0.
  • The effective force maGm a_GmaG​ is what the real forces actually produce. The inertia force is its reverse; it is the reversed effective force, not a real force.
  • Sign: FiF_iFi​ opposes aGa_GaG​ and TiT_iTi​ opposes α\alphaα. Get these directions right or the whole analysis is wrong.

Think of braking hard in a taxi on the Masaka road. The car decelerates backwards, and you are thrown forwards: the inertia force on you points opposite to the acceleration.

Effective force and inertia force

Effective force, maGm a_GmaG​

  • The net real force acting on the body.
  • Equals mass × acceleration of the centre of mass.
  • Points along the acceleration.

Inertia force, −maG-m a_G−maG​

  • The reverse of the effective force.
  • A fictitious force added for D'Alembert's method.
  • Points opposite the acceleration and makes ∑F=0\sum F = 0∑F=0.

Why it helps. With the inertia force included the body is in dynamic equilibrium, so force polygons and moment equations apply. Apply them link by link and the joint forces come out as in a static truss. The same free-body technique handles the whole mechanism at any instant; no separate dynamics machinery is needed.

2.2Static force analysis of mechanisms

Static equilibrium of a link

Ignoring inertia (at low speed, or as the static part of a superposition), each link is in equilibrium under the applied and constraint forces:

∑Fx=0,∑Fy=0,∑M=0\sum F_x = 0, \qquad \sum F_y = 0, \qquad \sum M = 0∑Fx​=0,∑Fy​=0,∑M=0three planar equilibrium equations per link
  • Applied forces: the useful load, gas pressure, the driving effort.
  • Constraint forces: the reactions at pins and sliding surfaces. A pin reaction has two unknown components; a slider reaction is normal to the sliding surface (one unknown, plus friction if included).
  • Three equations per link: solve link by link through the mechanism.

Two-force and three-force members

  • The force acts along the link: pure tension or compression.
  • A connecting rod, ignoring its own inertia, is the classic example.
  • Concurrency fixes the unknown directions; the closed triangle gives the magnitudes.

Four-force members and members with a torque

  • Real input links often carry two forces and a driving torque. The crank of an engine carries the rod force, the bearing reaction and the crankshaft torque.
  • Handle these by superposition: split into simpler two- and three-force cases and add.
  • Or resolve every force into components and apply ∑Fx\sum F_x∑Fx​, ∑Fy\sum F_y∑Fy​, ∑M\sum M∑M.
  • The input torque is usually the unknown we want.

Drawing the free-body diagrams

  1. Sketch each link separately with all the forces and torques on it.
  2. Mark joint reactions as equal-and-opposite pairs (third law).
  3. Identify the two-force members first: their force directions are known.
  4. Work from the link carrying the known load towards the input link.
  5. Apply ∑F=0\sum F = 0∑F=0 and ∑M=0\sum M = 0∑M=0, or draw a force polygon, for each link in turn.
  6. The last equation gives the required input force or torque.

Order matters: start where the load is known and march towards the input. In the wrong order each link has too many unknowns. Two-force members break the chain of unknowns.

Live model: static force analysis of a four-bar

A resisting torque T4T_4T4​ acts on the output link. The coupler is a two-force member, so its force lies along BC. One moment equation about O4O_4O4​ gives that force; one about O2O_2O2​ gives the input torque T2T_2T2​. Virtual work gives the same answer in one line.

Principle of virtual work

For a mechanism in equilibrium, the total work done by all the forces and torques in any small virtual displacement is zero:

∑F δs+∑T δθ=0\sum F\,\delta s + \sum T\,\delta\theta = 0∑Fδs+∑Tδθ=0virtual-work equilibrium condition
  • A fast route to the input force or torque without finding every joint reaction.
  • Use the velocity ratios from Chapter 1: δs\delta sδs and δθ\delta\thetaδθ are proportional to the known velocities. For the four-bar, T2 ω2+T4 ω4=0T_2\,\omega_2 + T_4\,\omega_4 = 0T2​ω2​+T4​ω4​=0.
  • Especially efficient when only the input-output relation is wanted.

Superposition and friction

Superposition

  • Analyse the effect of each load, and each inertia force, separately, then add.
  • Valid because the equilibrium equations are linear in the forces.
  • This is also how the static (load) analysis and the inertia analysis are combined.

Friction in mechanisms

  • A pin reaction is offset to touch the friction circle; a slider reaction tilts by the friction angle (Chapter 4).
  • Friction raises the required input effort and lowers the mechanical efficiency.
  • Usually a second-order correction: add it after the frictionless solution is understood.

2.3Inertia, mass moment of inertia and equivalent systems

Mass moment of inertia and radius of gyration

I=∑mr2=∫r2 dmI = \sum m r^2 = \int r^2\,dmI=∑mr2=∫r2dmmass moment of inertia: the resistance to angular acceleration
I=mk2I = m k^2I=mk2kkk, the radius of gyration: the radius at which the whole mass gives the same III
  • III depends on the axis: always state the axis.
  • Units: kg m². It plays the role in rotation that mass plays in translation.
  • Solid disc about its axis: I=12mr2I = \tfrac12 m r^2I=21​mr2. Thin ring: I=mr2I = m r^2I=mr2. The r2r^2r2 weighting is why flywheels put their mass in the rim.

Parallel-axis theorem

The moment of inertia about any axis equals that about a parallel axis through the centre of mass G, plus md2m d^2md2:

I=IG+md2,IG=mkG2I = I_G + m d^2, \qquad I_G = m k_G^2I=IG​+md2,IG​=mkG2​ddd: distance between the two parallel axes; kGk_GkG​: radius of gyration about G
  • Used to shift inertia from the centroidal axis to a pivot, for a link swinging about a pin: Ipin=IG+md2I_{pin} = I_G + m d^2Ipin​=IG​+md2.
  • For composite bodies, add the III of the parts, each transferred to the common axis.

Inertia torque of a rotating part

For a body turning about a fixed axis with angular acceleration α\alphaα:

Ti=−Iα,Tdrive=IαT_i = -I\alpha, \qquad T_{drive} = I\alphaTi​=−Iα,Tdrive​=Iαpure fixed-axis rotation
  • A large III needs a large torque to change ω\omegaω, so under a fluctuating torque ω\omegaω changes slowly. That is the whole principle of the flywheel, derived here.
  • For general plane motion, combine this with the inertia force Fi=−maGF_i = -m a_GFi​=−maG​.

Equivalent offset inertia force

Fi=−mfGF_i = -m f_GFi​=−mfG​fGf_GfG​: acceleration of the centre of mass
Ci=−IG αC_i = -I_G\,\alphaCi​=−IG​αIGI_GIG​: about G, perpendicular to the plane of motion

If ∑F\sum F∑F and ∑T\sum T∑T are the external forces and the torques about G, D'Alembert gives ∑F+Fi=0\sum F + F_i = 0∑F+Fi​=0 and ∑T+Ci=0\sum T + C_i = 0∑T+Ci​=0.

  • The force through G plus the couple can be replaced by one force FiF_iFi​, offset from G by h=IGα/(mfG)h = I_G\alpha/(m f_G)h=IG​α/(mfG​) so that its moment about G equals the inertia couple.
  • The magnitudes and directions of the inertia forces and couples are then known, and they are treated just like static loads. A dynamic analysis problem becomes a static one.

Dynamically equivalent two-mass system

  • Replace a link by two point masses so that the dynamics are unchanged.
  • Usually one mass is placed at the gudgeon (small-end) pin, the other near the crank pin.
  • This turns the connecting-rod inertia problem into two simple point masses: one that reciprocates with the piston, one that rotates with the crank pin.

Conditions for dynamic (kinetic) equivalence

Two masses m1m_1m1​, m2m_2m2​ at distances l1l_1l1​, l2l_2l2​ on either side of G are dynamically equivalent to the link when all three conditions hold:

m1+m2=mm1l1=m2l2m1l12+m2l22=IG=mkG2m_1 + m_2 = m \qquad m_1 l_1 = m_2 l_2 \qquad m_1 l_1^2 + m_2 l_2^2 = I_G = m k_G^2m1​+m2​=mm1​l1​=m2​l2​m1​l12​+m2​l22​=IG​=mkG2​same total mass, same centre of mass, same moment of inertia about G
  • Together these give l1l2=kG2l_1 l_2 = k_G^2l1​l2​=kG2​. Choose the first point (l1l_1l1​) and the second is fixed: l2=kG2/l1l_2 = k_G^2/l_1l2​=kG2​/l1​.
  • Matching only mass and centre of mass (two conditions) gives a statically equivalent system.

  • In practice the masses go at the two pin centres, the convenient points. Then l1l2≠kG2l_1 l_2 \ne k_G^2l1​l2​=kG2​ and only the first two conditions hold.
  • The inertia mismatch is put back as a correction couple. For a rod of length lll, G at distance bbb from the small end, and angular acceleration αc\alpha_cαc​:
ΔT=m b αc (l−L),L=b+kG2b\Delta T = m\,b\,\alpha_c\,(l - L), \qquad L = b + \frac{k_G^2}{b}ΔT=mbαc​(l−L),L=b+bkG2​​LLL: length of the equivalent simple pendulum swung from the small end

2.4Inertia forces in the slider-crank mechanism

Forces in the reciprocating engine mechanism

  • The gas pressure force on the piston drives the mechanism.
  • It is reacted by the connecting-rod thrust and the side thrust on the cylinder wall.
  • The rod thrust reaches the crank pin as the crank-pin force.
  • Its component perpendicular to the crank is the crank effort, which turns the shaft.

Force flow: gas force, then piston, then down the rod, then crank pin, then tangential crank effort, then shaft torque.

Velocity and acceleration of the piston

With crank radius rrr, angular velocity ω\omegaω, crank angle θ\thetaθ from TDC and obliquity ratio n=L/rn = L/rn=L/r:

vP=ωr(sin⁡θ+sin⁡2θ2n)v_P = \omega r\left(\sin\theta + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{2n}\right)vP​=ωr(sinθ+2nsin2θ​)piston velocity (approximate form)
aP=ω2r(cos⁡θ+cos⁡2θn)a_P = \omega^2 r\left(\cos\theta + \frac{\cos 2\theta}{n}\right)aP​=ω2r(cosθ+ncos2θ​)piston acceleration (approximate form)
  • The 1/n1/n1/n and 1/2n1/2n1/2n terms are the connecting-rod obliquity corrections; they vanish for a very long rod.
  • aPa_PaP​ is largest at TDC, ω2r(1+1/n)\omega^2 r(1 + 1/n)ω2r(1+1/n). That sets the peak reciprocating inertia force.

Reciprocating inertia force and piston effort

The reciprocating parts (piston, rings, gudgeon pin and the small-end share of the rod) of mass mRm_RmR​ resist the piston acceleration with an inertia force:

Fi=mR aP=mR ω2r(cos⁡θ+cos⁡2θn)F_i = m_R\,a_P = m_R\,\omega^2 r\left(\cos\theta + \frac{\cos 2\theta}{n}\right)Fi​=mR​aP​=mR​ω2r(cosθ+ncos2θ​)acts opposite to the piston acceleration (approximate form)

The piston effort FPF_PFP​ is the net force along the cylinder axis, pushing on the gudgeon pin:

FP=Fgas−Fi(± mRg for a vertical engine,− friction)F_P = F_{gas} - F_i \quad (\pm\, m_R g \text{ for a vertical engine}, -\,\text{friction})FP​=Fgas​−Fi​(±mR​g for a vertical engine,−friction)
  • FgasF_{gas}Fgas​ = gas pressure × piston area, read from the indicator diagram.
  • Near the dead centres inertia dominates. Early in the expansion stroke the inertia force subtracts from the gas force; late in the stroke it adds.

Thrust along the rod and on the cylinder wall

2.5Turning-moment (crank-effort) diagrams

The turning-moment diagram

Over one revolution the torque varies as

T=Ft r=F r(sin⁡θ+sin⁡2θ2n2−sin⁡2θ)T = F_t\,r = F\,r\left(\sin\theta + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{2\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2\theta}}\right)T=Ft​r=Fr(sinθ+2n2−sin2θ​sin2θ​)
  • FFF is the net piston effort; FtF_tFt​ is the crank effort.
  • The common plots are the turning-moment diagram (TTT against θ\thetaθ) and the crank-effort diagram (FtF_tFt​ against θ\thetaθ); one is the other times rrr.
  • The area under the TTT-θ\thetaθ curve is work. The mean torque line has the same area over a cycle: Tmean=T_{mean} =Tmean​= work per cycle ÷ angle of the cycle.

Single-cylinder four-stroke engine

  • The diagram repeats every two revolutions (a double-acting steam engine repeats every revolution).
  • Suction (0 to π): the turning moment is negative for most of the stroke and becomes positive near the end, as inertia drives the piston.
  • Compression (π to 2π): wholly negative; the flywheel pays.
  • Expansion (2π to 3π): positive throughout, with a tall peak early in the stroke.
  • Exhaust (3π to 4π): negative for most of the stroke.

See it in the live model below with "Single-cylinder four-stroke".

Multi-cylinder engines

  • The total crankshaft torque at any instant is the sum of the torques from each cylinder.
  • A two-cylinder engine with cranks at 90° varies less than a single cylinder; a three-cylinder engine with cranks at 120° varies less still.
  • Where the torque curve crosses the mean line from above, speed is at a maximum; where it crosses from below, speed is at a minimum.

2.6The flywheel

What a flywheel does

  • A flywheel controls the variation of speed within each cycle of an engine or machine. (A governor controls the mean speed over many cycles as the load changes.)
  • Fitted to the crankshaft, it makes the moment of inertia of the rotating parts large, so it acts as a reservoir of energy: it stores energy when the turning moment exceeds the load and gives it back when there is a shortage.

Let III be the moment of inertia of the flywheel, ω1\omega_1ω1​ and ω2\omega_2ω2​ the greatest and least speeds, ω\omegaω the mean speed, E=12Iω2E = \tfrac12 I\omega^2E=21​Iω2 the kinetic energy at mean speed and eee the maximum fluctuation of energy.

The flywheel for a punching press

  • In a press the motor torque is nearly constant, but the load torque comes in short, large bursts while the punch shears the plate.
  • The flywheel supplies the energy during the punch and the motor refills it during the rest of the cycle. The motor is then sized for the mean power, not the peak.
  • If punching takes a fraction fff of the cycle, the motor supplies fff of the punching energy during the punch, so the flywheel supplies e=Epunch(1−f)e = E_{punch}(1 - f)e=Epunch​(1−f).

2.7Summary

  • Dynamic forces come from accelerating masses; all machines have them while running.
  • D'Alembert: inertia forces and couples plus the external forces and torques give statical equilibrium. Then static methods apply.
  • An inertia force and couple can be replaced by one offset inertia force.
  • A rod can be replaced by two point masses with the same mass, centre of mass and moment of inertia. Placed at the two pin centres they need a correction couple.
  • Piston effort is the net force on the piston: FP=Fgas−mR ω2r(cos⁡θ+cos⁡2θ/n)F_P = F_{gas} - m_R\,\omega^2 r(\cos\theta + \cos 2\theta/n)FP​=Fgas​−mR​ω2r(cosθ+cos2θ/n).
  • Crank effort is the force at the crank pin perpendicular to the crank; times rrr it gives the turning moment T=FPr(sin⁡θ+sin⁡2θ/2n2−sin⁡2θ)T = F_P r(\sin\theta + \sin 2\theta/2\sqrt{n^2 - \sin^2\theta})T=FP​r(sinθ+sin2θ/2n2−sin2θ​).
  • The turning-moment diagram plots TTT against θ\thetaθ. The difference between the greatest and least flywheel energies is the maximum fluctuation of energy.
  • The flywheel limits the speed variation within a cycle: K=e/(Iω2)=e/2EK = e/(I\omega^2) = e/2EK=e/(Iω2)=e/2E. Rim speed is limited by σ=ρv2\sigma = \rho v^2σ=ρv2.
