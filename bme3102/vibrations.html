3.1Fundamentals of mechanical vibrations

Covered in this chapter

  • Work done in harmonic motion; elements of discrete (lumped-parameter) vibrating systems.
  • Undamped free vibration: the equilibrium, energy and Rayleigh methods.
  • Equivalent springs and dashpots; equivalent length of a shaft.
  • Damped vibration and the logarithmic decrement.
  • Forced vibration, vibration isolation and transmissibility.
  • Torsional vibration of single- and two-rotor systems.

Notation follows Rattan (2009): sss is the spring stiffness, ccc the damping coefficient, ξ\xiξ the damping ratio.

Definitions

A mechanical vibration is a periodic motion of a system about an equilibrium position, usually driven by elastic forces. The work done on the elastic element is stored as strain energy.

  • Free vibration: after the initial release there is no external force and no friction.
  • Damped vibration: the energy is gradually dissipated by friction and other resistances.
  • Forced vibration: a repeated force acts continuously; the system vibrates at the frequency of the force.
  • Period: time for the motion to repeat itself (s). Cycle: the motion completed in one period. Frequency: cycles per second (Hz).
  • Resonance: the forcing frequency equals the natural frequency of the system.

Types of vibration

  • Longitudinal: the shaft is stretched and shortened in turn. Particles move parallel to the axis; the stresses are tensile and compressive.
  • Transverse: the shaft bends one way and then the other. Particles move roughly perpendicular to the axis; bending stresses result.
  • Torsional: the shaft twists and untwists. Particles move in arcs about the axis; torsional shear stresses result.

Features of vibrating systems

A vibrating system needs inertia and a restoring element, and in practice some damping to dissipate energy:

  • Inertia elements: lumped masses for straight-line motion and lumped moments of inertia for angular motion.
  • Restoring elements: massless linear or torsional springs.
  • Damping elements: massless dampers (dashpots) that dissipate energy.

The degrees of freedom of a vibrating system are the number of independent coordinates needed to describe it: one for a mass on a spring or a simple pendulum, two for two masses on two springs or a double pendulum, and infinitely many for a beam with distributed mass.

3.4Damped vibration

Damping and the equation of motion

  • Damping is the dying out of vibration with time. It comes naturally from internal friction in the material and from the surrounding medium.
  • External damping is provided by dampers or dashpots: a piston in a cylinder of oil. Vehicle shock absorbers are the everyday example.
  • Viscous damping force = cx˙c\dot xcx˙, with ccc the damping coefficient (N s/m).

With the mass displaced xxx below the equilibrium position, inertia mx¨m\ddot xmx¨, damping cx˙c\dot xcx˙ and spring sxs xsx all act upwards:

3.5Forced vibration

Step input

  • A force is applied to the vibrating system. It may be a step, harmonic or periodic.
  • A step is a constant force applied suddenly. Its effect is like an added weight: the system vibrates about a new equilibrium position, F/sF/sF/s lower.
mx¨+sx=Fm\ddot x + s x = Fmx¨+sx=F

3.6Vibration isolation and transmissibility

Force transmitted to the foundation

  • Vibrating machines pass force into their foundations, and into the building. This is usually undesirable.
  • It is reduced by mounting the machine on springs, dampers or rubber pads.
  • Transmissibility ε\varepsilonε is the ratio of the force transmitted to the foundation to the force applied. It measures how well the isolation works.
