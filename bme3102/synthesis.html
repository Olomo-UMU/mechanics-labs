6.1Synthesis: designing a mechanism

Analysis and synthesis

  • Analysis (Chapters 1 and 2): the mechanism is given; find its motion and forces.
  • Synthesis: the motion is given; find a mechanism that produces it. Here the mechanism is a four-bar or a slider-crank, and we find its link lengths.
  • Chapter 1 counted links and joints (number synthesis). This chapter does dimensional synthesis: choosing the lengths and pivot positions.

Primary reading: Rattan (2009), chapter 5.

Three kinds of task

  • Function generation: the output angle must be a given function of the input angle, ϕ=f(θ)\phi = f(\theta)ϕ=f(θ). Example: a linkage that makes a fuel gauge pointer read the volume of an oddly shaped tank, or a mechanical computer of the old kind.
  • Path generation: a point on the coupler must trace a given path. Example: the film-advance claw of a projector, a walking machine's foot, the straight-line motions of Watt and Chebyshev.
  • Motion (rigid-body) generation: a body (the coupler) must pass through given positions, each with a given location and orientation. Example: a tipping bucket, a car bonnet hinge, a lift that moves a bag from the floor to a bench.

Precision points and structural error

  • A four-bar has only a few free dimensions, so it can match a function exactly at only a few points: the precision (accuracy) points. With the Freudenstein method below, three.
  • Between them the output differs from what is wanted: the structural error. It is zero at the precision points and largest between and beyond them.
  • The error depends on where the precision points are placed. Chebyshev spacing roughly equalises the error peaks, so it gives about the smallest maximum error for nnn points over xs≤x≤xfx_s \le x \le x_fxs​≤x≤xf​:
xj=xs+xf2−xf−xs2cos⁡(2j−1)π2n,j=1 to nx_j = \frac{x_s + x_f}{2} - \frac{x_f - x_s}{2}\cos\frac{(2j - 1)\pi}{2n}, \qquad j = 1 \text{ to } nxj​=2xs​+xf​​−2xf​−xs​​cos2n(2j−1)π​,j=1 to n

6.2Graphical synthesis

Motion generation: two and three positions

Choose two points B and C on the moving body as the coupler pins. Each pin moves on a circle about a fixed pivot:

  • Two positions: the fixed pivot A of pin B lies anywhere on the perpendicular bisector (midnormal) of B1B2B_1B_2B1​B2​; D lies anywhere on the midnormal of C1C2C_1C_2C1​C2​. There are infinitely many solutions.
  • Three positions: A is the centre of the circle through B1,B2,B3B_1, B_2, B_3B1​,B2​,B3​: the point where the midnormals of B1B2B_1B_2B1​B2​ and B2B3B_2B_3B2​B3​ meet. D likewise from C1,C2,C3C_1, C_2, C_3C1​,C2​,C3​. The solution is unique once B and C are chosen on the body.
  • Then check: does the linkage pass through the positions in the right order without changing branch, and are the transmission angles acceptable?

Live model: three-position motion generation

A body must pass through three given positions. Move the two hinge points on the body; the fixed pivots A and D are found as circle centres, and the linkage runs through the positions.

Try it: move a hinge until the three positions of that pin almost fall on a line. The pivot shoots off to a great distance: that design is useless. Look also for the "order defect", when the linkage reaches the positions in the wrong order.

Live model: crank-rocker design

Set the rocker swing and the time ratio, then slide the crank pivot round its circle. The model gives the link lengths, checks Grashof and the transmission angle, and runs the linkage.

Try it: the time ratio and swing are met everywhere on the circle, but the transmission angle is not. Find the position with the best minimum transmission angle; designers usually want at least 40°.

3D lab: build and test a four-bar

Take the link lengths from your design (or from the crank-rocker model above), set them on the kit and turn the crank: check that it is a crank-rocker, read the transmission angle round the cycle and look at the coupler curve.

6.3Analytical synthesis: Freudenstein's equation

Deriving Freudenstein's equation

Put the fixed link AD of length ddd along the xxx axis, the input AB (length aaa) at angle θ\thetaθ, the coupler BC (bbb) at β\betaβ, and the output DC (ccc) at ϕ\phiϕ. The loop closes:

acos⁡θ+bcos⁡β=d+ccos⁡ϕ,asin⁡θ+bsin⁡β=csin⁡ϕa\cos\theta + b\cos\beta = d + c\cos\phi, \qquad a\sin\theta + b\sin\beta = c\sin\phiacosθ+bcosβ=d+ccosϕ,asinθ+bsinβ=csinϕ

Path generation and coupler curves

  • A point on the coupler traces a coupler curve; a four-bar can produce a great variety, from near-ellipses to figure eights and curves with straight portions (see the four-bar explorer in Chapter 1).
  • Path synthesis picks link lengths so that the coupler curve passes through given points. Hrones and Nelson published an atlas of some 7000 coupler curves that designers used to choose from.
  • Roberts-Chebyshev theorem: every coupler curve can be traced by three different four-bars, the cognates. If the first design has poor transmission angles, a cognate may not.
  • With more precision points (four or five) the equations become nonlinear and are solved numerically, usually with complex-number (loop) methods.
