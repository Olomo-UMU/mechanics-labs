Kinematics, kinetics and dynamics

Kinematics

  • Motion without reference to the forces that cause it.
  • Displacement, velocity and acceleration.
  • "Geometry of motion": position, velocity and acceleration diagrams.
  • This chapter is almost entirely kinematics.

Kinetics

  • Relates the forces to the resulting motion.
  • Uses mass, inertia, torque, and Newton's and D'Alembert's laws.
  • Chapter 2 onward (dynamics of machines).

Dynamics = kinematics + kinetics. We analyse the geometry first, without mass and forces, then bring in mass and force. A velocity diagram is kinematics; a turning-moment diagram is kinetics.

Links and the rigid-body idealisation

  • A link is a resistant body forming part of a machine and connecting other links.
  • It is idealised as rigid: the distances between its points do not change during motion.
  • Links are named by their number of nodes: binary (2), ternary (3), quaternary (4).
  • A body that resists in one sense only (a belt in tension, a fluid column in compression) is still a link.

The rigid-body assumption is what makes velocity and acceleration analysis tractable.

Kinematic pairs: lower and higher

  • A kinematic pair is two links joined so that their relative motion is constrained.
  • Lower pair: contact over a surface, low wear, load spread over an area.
  • Higher pair: contact along a line or at a point; it allows more complex motion.
  • A planar lower pair removes 2 degrees of freedom; a planar higher pair removes 1.

This distinction drives wear, contact stress and the constraint count in the mobility equation.

The lower pairs and their freedoms

PairRelative motionFreedomsExample
Revolute (R)turning about an axis1pin joint, crank on its bearing
Prismatic (P)sliding along an axis1piston in a cylinder
Screw (H)turning and sliding, linked by the lead1lead screw of a lathe, screw jack

Only R and P appear in planar mechanisms. C, S, H and the planar pair belong to spatial mechanisms (section 1.3).

Three ways to classify a pair

By contact and relative motion

  • Lower or higher pair (surface, or line and point contact).
  • Turning, sliding, rolling, screw or spherical motion.
  • Governs contact stress and wear.

By the nature of the constraint

  • Closed (self-closed): the shape keeps the contact, as with a pin in a hole.
  • Unclosed (force-closed): a force keeps the contact, as with a spring-loaded cam follower.
  • Matters for higher pairs that can separate.

A cam follower is force-closed. If the spring is too weak at speed, the follower "jumps" and loses contact with the cam.

Degrees of freedom of a pair

  • An unconstrained rigid body has 3 degrees of freedom in a plane (two translations, one rotation) and 6 in space.
  • A pair removes freedoms; the number removed is its constraint.
  • A planar revolute or prismatic pair leaves 1 freedom and removes 2.
  • A planar higher pair (cam, gear teeth) leaves 2 freedoms and removes 1.
  • This bookkeeping is exactly what the mobility equation counts.

Chain, mechanism, machine, structure

  • Kinematic chain: links joined so that their relative motion is definite.
  • Fix one link and the chain becomes a mechanism.
  • A machine transmits force and does work. Mechanism = motion; machine = motion + force.
  • A structure has zero mobility.

1.2Mobility: degrees of freedom

Kutzbach's mobility criterion

For a planar mechanism with NNN links (including the fixed link), P1P_1P1​ lower pairs (1 freedom each) and P2P_2P2​ higher pairs (2 freedoms each):

F=3(N−1)−2P1−P2F = 3(N-1) - 2P_1 - P_2F=3(N−1)−2P1​−P2​planar degrees of freedom (mobility)
  • 3(N−1)3(N-1)3(N−1): the free planar freedoms of the moving links. Start from 3N3N3N for NNN links and fix one (minus 3).
  • Each lower pair removes 2 freedoms; each higher pair removes 1.
  • FFF is the number of independent inputs the mechanism needs.

Reading the mobility number

MobilityNameMeaning
F>0F > 0F>0mechanismFFF independent inputs give definite motion. F=1F = 1F=1 is the usual single-input machine.
F=0F = 0F=0structureno mobility: a statically determinate rigid frame
F<0F < 0F<0preloaded frameover-constrained, statically indeterminate

F=1F = 1F=1 is the design target for most machines: one motor, definite motion.

Grübler's criterion

For a planar mechanism with only lower pairs and a single degree of freedom, set P2=0P_2 = 0P2​=0 and F=1F = 1F=1 in Kutzbach's equation:

3N−2P1−4=03N - 2P_1 - 4 = 03N−2P1​−4=0condition for a constrained single-freedom planar linkage
  • A quick check that an all-lower-pair linkage has F=1F = 1F=1.
  • Satisfied by the four-bar and the slider-crank (N=4N = 4N=4, P1=4P_1 = 4P1​=4).

Number synthesis: how many links and pairs

  • For F=1F = 1F=1 with only lower pairs, 3N−2P1−4=03N - 2P_1 - 4 = 03N−2P1​−4=0, so NNN must be even.
  • N=4N = 4N=4, P1=4P_1 = 4P1​=4: the four-bar and slider-crank families (one loop).
  • N=6N = 6N=6, P1=7P_1 = 7P1​=7: Watt's and Stephenson's six-bar chains (two loops). They differ in how the two ternary links are placed.
  • Number synthesis fixes how many links and joints are needed before choosing dimensions. Type and dimensional synthesis (choosing pairs and lengths) follow later in the course.

Traps in the count

  • Redundant (idle) freedom: a roller free to spin on its pin adds a freedom that does not affect the output. Subtract it.
  • Redundant constraint: a duplicate (parallel) link makes Kutzbach read low; the real FFF is higher.
  • Coincident joints: where three links share one pin there are two pairs, not one.
  • Always check the count against physical sense.

Locomotive coupling rods on three axles are the classic redundant-constraint case.

1.3Kinematic chains, mechanisms and inversions

The four-bar (quadric-cycle) chain

  • Four links and four revolute pairs: the simplest closed single-freedom chain.
  • Frame (fixed), crank (input), coupler (floating), rocker (output).
  • A point on the coupler traces a coupler curve, used in path synthesis.
  • The transmission angle μ\muμ, between coupler and rocker, measures how effectively force reaches the output.
  • Real examples: windscreen wiper drive, locking pliers, the beam of a pumpjack.

Grashof's law

With sss the shortest link, lll the longest, and ppp, qqq the other two, at least one link can make full revolutions relative to another if

s+l≤p+qs + l \le p + qs+l≤p+qGrashof condition
  • Grashof chain (s+l<p+qs + l < p + qs+l<p+q): at least one link makes complete revolutions.
  • Non-Grashof chain (s+l>p+qs + l > p + qs+l>p+q): no link can rotate fully; every inversion is a triple-rocker.
  • Equality gives a change-point linkage, which can lock or switch branch at the flat position.
  • Grashof decides whether continuous motor input is possible at all.

Grashof classes of the four-bar

For a Grashof chain the class depends on which link is fixed:

  • Crank-rocker: the shortest link is the crank (next to the frame). The crank rotates, the rocker oscillates.
  • Double-crank (drag-link): the shortest link is the frame. Both side links rotate fully.
  • Double-rocker: the shortest link is the coupler. Both side links oscillate, but the coupler turns fully.

The same four lengths give different machines depending on which link is grounded. That is inversion.

Inversion: the idea

  • Inversion: fixing (grounding) a different link of the same kinematic chain.
  • The relative motion between links is unchanged; only the absolute motions change.
  • A chain of NNN links gives NNN inversions, some of which may be identical.
  • Think of it as watching the mechanism from a camera fixed to a different link.

Inversions of the four-bar chain

  • Fix the frame: the ordinary crank-rocker or drag-link.
  • Beam engine: a crank-rocker inversion (rotary to rocking beam motion).
  • Coupling rod of a locomotive: a drag-link (parallelogram) keeping the driving wheels in step.
  • Watt's straight-line and indicator mechanisms: double-rocker inversions.
  • All share one four-revolute chain.

The slider-crank chain

  • Four links: three revolute pairs and one prismatic pair, so still N=4N = 4N=4, P1=4P_1 = 4P1​=4, F=1F = 1F=1.
  • Converts rotary crank motion into reciprocating slider motion.
  • The heart of every reciprocating engine, pump and compressor.
  • It is a four-bar whose rocker is infinitely long: the prismatic pair replaces one revolute pair.

The four inversions of the slider-crank

  • First (fix the frame): reciprocating engine or compressor, rotary to and from reciprocating.
  • Second (fix the crank): Whitworth quick-return and the rotary (Gnome) aero engine.
  • Third (fix the connecting rod): oscillating-cylinder engine; crank and slotted-lever quick-return.
  • Fourth (fix the slider): the hand pump or pendulum pump.

Quick-return mechanisms

  • A quick-return cuts on a slow forward stroke and returns fast, with no cutting on the return. A fast return raises output on a shaping machine.
  • Whitworth: the crank is fixed; a pin slides in a rotating slotted link.
  • Crank and slotted lever: the connecting rod is fixed; the crank pin slides in an oscillating lever.
  • The ratio of stroke times equals the ratio of the crank angles α:β\alpha : \betaα:β turned during the two strokes.

The double slider-crank chain

  • Two prismatic pairs and two revolute pairs.
  • Elliptical trammel: a point on the coupler traces an ellipse.
  • Scotch yoke: gives exact simple harmonic motion, x=rcos⁡θx = r\cos\thetax=rcosθ.
  • Oldham coupling: joins parallel offset shafts at a constant speed ratio. It is the practical one, because it accepts shaft misalignment.

Transmission of torque and force; mechanical advantage

  • Transmission angle μ\muμ: the angle between the coupler and the output link at the output pin.
  • The force component that turns the output is proportional to sin⁡μ\sin\musinμ. It is best near μ=90∘\mu = 90^\circμ=90∘; keep 40∘≤μ≤140∘40^\circ \le \mu \le 140^\circ40∘≤μ≤140∘.
  • Mechanical advantage = output force ÷ input force.
  • At a toggle position the input crank and coupler fall in line and the mechanical advantage tends to infinity. Clamps, stone crushers and locking pliers use this.
  • Poor μ\muμ gives sluggish, force-wasting motion and possible jamming.

At a toggle a small input gives a huge output force, but the mechanism is close to a singular position. Read off the minimum transmission angle in the four-bar explorer above.

Equivalent linkages

  • A higher pair can be replaced, at an instant, by an equivalent lower-pair linkage.
  • The equivalent link joins the two centres of curvature of the contacting surfaces.
  • This lets a cam or a pair of gear teeth be analysed at an instant with four-bar methods.
  • It is valid only at that instant; recompute as the surfaces roll on.

Spatial mechanisms and manipulators

  • Planar analysis assumes all motion lies in parallel planes; most machines qualify.
  • Spatial mechanisms move in three dimensions and use C, S and H pairs.
  • Examples: the universal (Hooke's) joint, the RSSR crank, spatial cam mechanisms.
  • Spatial mobility counts 6 freedoms per body: F=6(N−1)−∑(6−fi)F = 6(N-1) - \sum (6 - f_i)F=6(N−1)−∑(6−fi​), summing over each pair with fif_ifi​ freedoms.
  • A robot arm is an open spatial chain. A six-axis arm has six 1-freedom joints, so F=6F = 6F=6, with one motor per joint.

1.4Velocity analysis

Velocity of a point on a moving link

For two points A and B on the same rigid link turning with angular velocity ω\omegaω:

v⃗B=v⃗A+v⃗BA\vec v_B = \vec v_A + \vec v_{BA}vB​=vA​+vBA​relative velocity equation
∣v⃗BA∣=ω⋅AB,v⃗BA⊥AB|\vec v_{BA}| = \omega \cdot AB, \qquad \vec v_{BA} \perp AB∣vBA​∣=ω⋅AB,vBA​⊥ABthe relative velocity is perpendicular to the link

Instantaneous centres and Kennedy's theorem

  • The instantaneous centre (I-centre) of two links is the point where they have no relative velocity at that instant.
  • A mechanism of nnn links has N=n(n−1)/2N = n(n-1)/2N=n(n−1)/2 I-centres. The four-bar has 6.
  • Aronhold-Kennedy theorem: the three I-centres of any three links lie on a straight line.
  • A hidden I-centre is found where two Kennedy lines cross. For the four-bar, I13I_{13}I13​ lies on line I12I23I_{12}I_{23}I12​I23​ and on line I14I34I_{14}I_{34}I14​I34​.

Centripetal and tangential acceleration

  • Centripetal: an=ω2⋅ABa^n = \omega^2 \cdot ABan=ω2⋅AB, directed from B towards A.

  • ana^nan exists whenever ω≠0\omega \ne 0ω=0, even at constant speed; ata^tat only when α≠0\alpha \ne 0α=0.
  • The total relative acceleration is the vector sum of the two.

When does the Coriolis component appear?

It is present when

  • A point slides along a link that is rotating.
  • Slotted-lever and Whitworth quick-return mechanisms.
  • Any sliding pair on a rotating link.

It is not present when

  • Both points are fixed on the same rigid link.
  • The sliding is along a link that only translates.
  • ω\omegaω of the guiding link is zero at that instant.

The test: is a point sliding along a rotating link? If yes, add 2ωv2\omega v2ωv. This prevents the commonest error in acceleration analysis.

Coriolis acceleration component

ac=2 ω va_c = 2\,\omega\, vac​=2ωvω\omegaω: angular velocity of the guiding link; vvv: sliding velocity along it
  • It arises when a point slides along a link that is itself rotating.
  • Direction: turn the sliding velocity vvv through 90° in the sense of ω\omegaω.
  • It is often the largest term in quick-return mechanisms, and the easiest to forget. Go back to the slotted-lever model in section 1.3: the red arrow is 2ωv2\omega v2ωv. It vanishes at the two extreme positions of the lever, where the lever's angular velocity is momentarily zero, and it flips over when the block reverses its sliding direction.

  • A graphical shortcut for the velocity and acceleration of the slider in a slider-crank.
  • The crank OA itself, drawn to scale, represents ωr\omega rωr (velocity) and ω2r\omega^2 rω2r (acceleration).

Q2A Grashof four-bar has its shortest link fixed. What do you get?

  1. Crank-rocker
  2. Double-crank (drag-link)
  3. Double-rocker
  4. Triple-rocker

With the shortest link as the frame, both links next to it can turn fully: a drag-link.

  4. Slider fixed

Fixing the slider (piston) makes the cylinder move relative to it: the pendulum pump.
