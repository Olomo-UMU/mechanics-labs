5.1Gears and their terms

What this chapter covers

  • Classification of gears; gear terminology; the law of gearing.
  • Involute and cycloidal profiles; path, arc and ratio of contact.
  • Interference and the minimum number of teeth.
  • Helical, bevel and worm gears in brief.
  • Simple, compound, reverted and epicyclic gear trains; torques in epicyclic trains; the differential.

Primary reading: Rattan (2009), chapters 10 and 11.

Why toothed gears

  • Friction wheels and belts can slip, so their speed ratio is not exact. Teeth make the drive positive: the speed ratio is fixed by the tooth numbers.
  • Gears carry large torques in a small space, at high efficiency (98 to 99% per spur mesh).
  • Against that, they cost more, need accurate centre distances and lubrication, and are noisy at high speed if poorly made.

Gears are also classed by the type of mesh (external, internal, rack) and by peripheral speed: low (below 3 m/s), medium (3 to 15 m/s) and high (above 15 m/s).

Gear terminology

  • Pitch circle: the imaginary circle that rolls without slip on the pitch circle of the mating gear. Its diameter is ddd.
  • Pitch point: where the two pitch circles touch.
  • Module m=d/Tm = d/Tm=d/T (mm), with TTT the number of teeth. Meshing gears must have the same module.
  • Circular pitch p=πd/T=πmp = \pi d/T = \pi mp=πd/T=πm: the arc from a point on one tooth to the same point on the next.
  • Addendum: radial height above the pitch circle, usually 1m1m1m. Dedendum: depth below it, usually 1.25m1.25m1.25m. The difference is the clearance.
  • Pressure angle ϕ\phiϕ: between the line of action and the common tangent at the pitch point. Standard values 14.5°, 20° and 25°.
  • Base circle: rb=rcos⁡ϕr_b = r\cos\phirb​=rcosϕ, the circle from which the involute is generated.

The law of gearing

For a constant speed ratio, the common normal to the tooth profiles at the point of contact must always pass through a fixed point, the pitch point P, on the line of centres:

ω1ω2=O2PO1P=r2r1=T2T1\frac{\omega_1}{\omega_2} = \frac{O_2P}{O_1P} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{T_2}{T_1}ω2​ω1​​=O1​PO2​P​=r1​r2​​=T1​T2​​velocity ratio of a pair of gears
  • Profiles that obey the law are conjugate. Involute and cycloidal profiles are the two used in practice.
  • Unless contact is at P, the teeth slide on each other. The velocity of sliding at a contact point C is
vs=(ω1+ω2)⋅PC(external gears),vs=(ω1−ω2)⋅PC(internal)v_s = (\omega_1 + \omega_2)\cdot PC \quad\text{(external gears)}, \qquad v_s = (\omega_1 - \omega_2)\cdot PC \quad\text{(internal)}vs​=(ω1​+ω2​)⋅PC(external gears),vs​=(ω1​−ω2​)⋅PC(internal)

Sliding is zero at the pitch point and greatest at the start and end of contact. That is where wear and scoring happen.

Involute and cycloidal profiles

Involute

  • The curve traced by the end of a taut string unwound from the base circle.
  • The line of action is a straight line, the common tangent to the two base circles, so the pressure angle is constant.
  • The speed ratio stays exact if the centre distance changes a little.
  • One rack cutter cuts every tooth number of a given module. Used for nearly all power gears.

Cycloidal

  • Traced by a point on a circle rolling on the pitch circle.
  • No interference, and a convex face meets a concave flank, so wear is lower.
  • The centre distance must be exact, and the pressure angle varies.
  • Used in clocks, watches and some instrument gearing.

5.2Contact of involute teeth

Path of contact

Contact runs along the line of action. It starts where the wheel's addendum circle cuts the line (K) and ends where the pinion's addendum circle cuts it (L). With pitch radii rrr (pinion), RRR (wheel) and addendum radii rar_ara​, RaR_aRa​:

KP=Ra2−R2cos⁡2ϕ−Rsin⁡ϕ,PL=ra2−r2cos⁡2ϕ−rsin⁡ϕKP = \sqrt{R_a^2 - R^2\cos^2\phi} - R\sin\phi, \qquad PL = \sqrt{r_a^2 - r^2\cos^2\phi} - r\sin\phiKP=Ra2​−R2cos2ϕ​−Rsinϕ,PL=ra2​−r2cos2ϕ​−rsinϕpath of approach and path of recess
path of contact=KP+PL,arc of contact=path of contactcos⁡ϕ\text{path of contact} = KP + PL, \qquad \text{arc of contact} = \frac{\text{path of contact}}{\cos\phi}path of contact=KP+PL,arc of contact=cosϕpath of contact​

Contact ratio and angle of action

contact ratio=arc of contactp=KP+PLpcos⁡ϕ=KP+PLpb\text{contact ratio} = \frac{\text{arc of contact}}{p} = \frac{KP + PL}{p\cos\phi} = \frac{KP + PL}{p_b}contact ratio=parc of contact​=pcosϕKP+PL​=pb​KP+PL​pb=pcos⁡ϕp_b = p\cos\phipb​=pcosϕ: the base pitch, the spacing of teeth along the line of action
  • The contact ratio is the average number of pairs of teeth in contact. It must exceed 1, or there are moments with no tooth driving; aim for 1.2 or more.
  • The angle of action of a gear is the angle it turns through while one pair is in contact: arc of contact ÷ pitch radius.

Interference

  • The involute exists only outside the base circle. If the tip of the wheel tooth reaches inside the pinion's base circle, it meets the non-involute part of the pinion flank and digs in: interference.
  • The points where the line of action touches the base circles (T1T_1T1​ on the pinion, T2T_2T2​ on the wheel) are the interference points. The path of contact must stay between them.
  • Cut gears avoid it by undercutting the pinion flank, which weakens the tooth at its root.
Ra,max⁡=R2cos⁡2ϕ+(r+R)2sin⁡2ϕR_{a,\max} = \sqrt{R^2\cos^2\phi + (r + R)^2\sin^2\phi}Ra,max​=R2cos2ϕ+(r+R)2sin2ϕ​largest wheel addendum radius free of interference (swap rrr and RRR for the pinion)

Helical, bevel and worm gears

  • Helical: teeth cut at a helix angle ψ\psiψ to the axis. Contact spreads gradually across the face, so they run quieter and carry more. Normal module mn=mcos⁡ψm_n = m\cos\psimn​=mcosψ. They push sideways: axial thrust Fa=Fttan⁡ψF_a = F_t\tan\psiFa​=Ft​tanψ, taken by thrust bearings or cancelled with double-helical (herringbone) teeth.
  • Bevel: cone-shaped gears for intersecting shafts, usually at 90°. Mitre gears are bevel gears of equal size.
  • Worm and wheel: a screw (worm) drives a wheel on a skew shaft. Velocity ratio = teeth on wheel ÷ starts on worm, so 40 to 1 in one stage is common. Its efficiency follows the screw formula of Chapter 4, η=tan⁡α/tan⁡(α+ϕ)\eta = \tan\alpha/\tan(\alpha + \phi)η=tanα/tan(α+ϕ); a single-start worm is often self-locking, which suits hoists.

5.3Gear trains

Simple and compound trains

Simple train

One gear on each shaft. Intermediate (idler) gears change the direction, not the ratio:

NlastNfirst=±TfirstTlast\frac{N_{last}}{N_{first}} = \pm\frac{T_{first}}{T_{last}}Nfirst​Nlast​​=±Tlast​Tfirst​​

An odd number of idlers keeps the direction; an even number reverses it.

Compound train

Two or more gears fixed on an intermediate shaft give a large ratio in a small space:

train value=NlastNfirst=product of teeth on driversproduct of teeth on driven\text{train value} = \frac{N_{last}}{N_{first}} = \frac{\text{product of teeth on drivers}}{\text{product of teeth on driven}}train value=Nfirst​Nlast​​=product of teeth on drivenproduct of teeth on drivers​

A reverted train is a compound train whose first and last gears are coaxial, as in a lathe back gear or a clock. Equal centre distances give r1+r2=r3+r4r_1 + r_2 = r_3 + r_4r1​+r2​=r3​+r4​, and with one module T1+T2=T3+T4T_1 + T_2 = T_3 + T_4T1​+T2​=T3​+T4​.

Epicyclic gear trains

  • At least one gear axis moves: a planet carried on an arm (carrier) rolls around a sun gear, often inside an internal annulus.
  • Large ratios in a compact, coaxial package, with the load shared by several planets. Used in automatic gearboxes, hub gears, hoists, wind-turbine gearboxes and the car differential.
  • Analyse by the tabular method: first fix the arm and turn one gear +x+x+x revolutions; then add +y+y+y to everything (which turns the whole train as a block). Choose xxx and yyy to meet the given conditions.

Torques in an epicyclic train

With no friction and no acceleration, the torques on the three members balance, and the net power is zero:

T1+T2+T3=0,T1N1+T2N2+T3N3=0T_1 + T_2 + T_3 = 0, \qquad T_1 N_1 + T_2 N_2 + T_3 N_3 = 0T1​+T2​+T3​=0,T1​N1​+T2​N2​+T3​N3​=0
  • The fixed member (N=0N = 0N=0) needs a holding (fixing) torque, which the casing or a brake band must supply. In an automatic gearbox, the brake bands that hold the annulus are sized from this torque.

The differential

  • On a bend the outer wheel of a car travels further than the inner one. A solid axle would force one tyre to scrub.
  • The crown wheel carries the cage (arm) with bevel planets meshing with two bevel suns, one on each half-shaft. Equal sun teeth give, by the tabular method,
Ncage=Nleft+Nright2N_{cage} = \frac{N_{left} + N_{right}}{2}Ncage​=2Nleft​+Nright​​

With the car in gear (cage held) and both driving wheels jacked up, turning one wheel makes the other turn the opposite way at the same speed. On mud the wheel with less grip spins at twice the cage speed while the other stops, which is why tractors have a differential lock.
