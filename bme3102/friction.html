4.1Friction

Covered in this chapter

  • Types of friction; dry friction; angle of repose and friction angle; motion along an inclined plane.
  • Friction of nuts and screws; the screw jack.
  • Pivot and collar friction; thrust bearings.
  • Friction clutches: single-plate, multi-plate, cone and centrifugal.
  • Rolling-contact bearings; greasy (boundary) friction at a journal.

Primary reading: Rattan (2009), chapter 8.

Introduction

  • Friction is the resistance to relative motion of two surfaces in contact, arising from the irregularities of the surfaces.
  • The friction force is parallel to the surfaces and opposes the sliding.
  • It can be useful or harmful. Clutches, brakes, belt drives and bolted joints need enough friction to work. In journal bearings and gears, friction wastes power and makes heat.
  • So in design we sometimes need more friction and sometimes less.

A bolt's tightening torque mostly fights friction: typically only about 10% of it stretches the bolt, the rest is lost under the head and in the threads. That is why torque settings assume a stated lubrication.

Kinds of friction

  • Dry friction: relative motion between completely unlubricated surfaces.
    • Solid (sliding) friction: the surfaces slide over each other.
    • Rolling friction: one surface rolls over another, as in ball and roller bearings.
  • Skin, greasy or boundary friction: a very thin layer of lubricant separates the surfaces, but high spots break through and touch.
  • Film (viscous) friction: the surfaces are completely separated by lubricant, and friction comes from shearing the oil layers.

Laws of solid friction

Experiments show that the force of solid friction

  • is directly proportional to the normal reaction between the surfaces,
  • opposes the motion between the surfaces,
  • depends on the materials of the two surfaces,
  • is independent of the area of contact,
  • is independent of the sliding velocity (within limits).

4.3Pivots and collars

Collar and pivot bearings

  • The axial (thrust) force on a rotating shaft is taken by a pivot or a collar.
  • Collar bearing: a collar anywhere along the shaft bears the axial load on a mating surface. The surface may be flat or conical.
  • Pivot (footstep) bearing: the end of the shaft sits in a recess and bears the thrust there. Again flat or conical.
  • Examples: the thrust collar of a vertical turbine pump, the footstep bearing of a hand-operated maize mill, a ship's propeller thrust block.

Which theory to use

  • The uniform-pressure theory always gives the larger friction torque. The true value lies between the two.
  • Clutches transmit torque by friction. A worn clutch surely transmits the uniform-wear torque (the lower value), but not necessarily the uniform-pressure torque. So design a clutch on uniform wear: it is the safe side. The actual torque will be a little higher.
  • Bearings: friction is a loss. Calculate the power lost on uniform pressure, again the safe side; the actual loss will be a little less.

4.4Friction clutches

What a clutch does

  • A clutch connects or disconnects two coaxial shafts at will, while running.
  • In a friction clutch the engine shaft drives the gearbox shaft through friction between two or more rotating surfaces pressed together.
  • When engaged the surfaces are clamped firmly and the clutch turns as one unit. If the resisting torque on the driven shaft exceeds the clutch's capacity, the clutch slips.

Single-plate (disc) clutch

  • The clutch plate (driven disc) sits on a splined hub that slides along the splines of the driven shaft. It is steel with a ring of friction lining on each side.
  • The flywheel is fixed to the engine shaft. A spring-loaded pressure plate clamps the clutch plate against the flywheel.
  • Flywheel, cover and pressure plate all turn with the engine. The pedal moves the pressure plate through a thrust (release) bearing.
  • Pedal down: the release levers pull the pressure plate back, the linings run free, and the driven shaft stops.
  • Pedal up: the springs clamp the plate between flywheel and pressure plate, and friction on both faces drives the shaft.

Multi-plate clutch

  • More plates and friction linings raise the torque capacity without a larger diameter. The number of friction rings depends on the torque to be carried.
  • Friction rings splined on their outer edge engage splines in the flywheel housing and slide axially; they turn with the engine. Discs splined on their inner edge turn with the driven shaft.
  • When the pedal force is removed, a spring presses the discs and rings together and the torque passes from engine shaft to driven shaft.
  • Used in motorcycles, where the clutch must be small, often running in oil (wet clutch).

Cone clutch

  • The contact surfaces are cones. A spring keeps cone B pressed into cone A; to disengage, B is pulled back by levers against the spring.
  • Advantage: the wedge action raises the normal force for a given axial force.

Centrifugal clutch

  • The driving member carries sliding shoes (often four), held in by flat springs. The outer faces of the shoes carry friction lining.
  • As speed rises, the centrifugal force on the shoes grows. When it beats the springs, the shoes move out and press on the inside of the drum (rim), and torque passes to it.
  • So the clutch engages only when the motor has enough speed to take the load: used on mopeds, chainsaws and small boda-boda style scooters.

4.5Anti-friction (rolling-contact) bearings

Why rolling bearings

  • A shaft turning in a plain bearing slides. With balls or rollers between shaft and housing, the contact rolls instead, between the rollers and each race.
  • Balls, rollers and races are made of hardened chromium steel or chrome-nickel steel.
  • Rolling friction is far lower than sliding friction at start-up, so rolling bearings start easily under load.

Ball bearings

  • Hardened balls run between two hardened races. The inner race is fitted to the shaft; the outer race is a tight fit in the housing. So there is no relative motion between shaft and inner race, or between outer race and housing.
  • The races have shallow grooves of slightly larger radius than the balls.
  • A light brass or pressed-steel cage keeps the balls evenly spaced.
  • Balls and races are very hard, so they distort little and the rolling friction is very low.
  • Friction is slightly higher when lubricated than when dry, but a little lubricant is still needed to stop rusting and to carry away heat.

Roller bearings

  • Hardened cylindrical rollers replace the balls: line contact instead of point contact, so they carry heavier loads.
  • A plain cylindrical roller bearing carries only radial load, perpendicular to the shaft.
  • In one common type the outer race is plain and the inner race is grooved to locate the rollers, with a cage keeping them apart.
  • Small rollers used without a cage are needle rollers: high load capacity in a small radial space.
  • Cylindrical roller bearings do not suit misaligned shafts; the rollers then load one edge and fail early.

4.6Greasy friction at a journal

Greasy (boundary) friction

  • Wet two metal surfaces with a little lubricant and a film of molecular thickness forms on each. It clings to the surface and is called an adsorbed film.
  • With these films in place the coefficient of friction is far lower than for dry surfaces.
  • The ability of a lubricant to form such a film is its oiliness. Of two oils with equal viscosity, the one that cuts friction more has the greater oiliness.
  • When the surfaces are wetted with only this very thin layer and high spots still touch, the friction is greasy or boundary friction. It obeys laws like those of dry friction.

The journal at rest and turning

  • In heavily loaded, slow-running bearings boundary friction applies, and the friction force is taken as proportional to the normal reaction.
  • At rest, the shaft's weight WWW acts through its centre, and the bearing reaction acts straight up in line with it. The shaft rests at the bottom, A, metal to metal.
  • When a torque turns the shaft, the contact point creeps up the bearing, against the direction of rotation, to B. The common normal at B passes through the shaft centre.
  • At B act the normal reaction RnR_nRn​ and the friction μRn\mu R_nμRn​ along the tangent. Their resultant RRR leans ϕ\phiϕ from the normal.

The friction circle

In equilibrium the resultant RRR must be vertical and equal to WWW. WWW and RRR are parallel but offset, so they form a couple. With OCOCOC the perpendicular from the centre O to the line of RRR:

Tf=W⋅OC=Wrsin⁡ϕT_f = W\cdot OC = W r\sin\phiTf​=W⋅OC=Wrsinϕfor small ϕ\phiϕ, sin⁡ϕ\sin\phisinϕ is about tan⁡ϕ=μ\tan\phi = \mutanϕ=μ, so TfT_fTf​ is about μWr\mu W rμWr
  • This friction couple equals and opposes the couple that turns the shaft.
  • A circle of radius OC=rsin⁡ϕOC = r\sin\phiOC=rsinϕ about O is the friction circle of the journal. The effect of friction is to shift the reaction so that it touches the friction circle.
  • The same idea gives the pin friction in linkages (Chapter 2): the joint force is drawn tangent to the friction circle of each pin.

Q1A block just slides when the plane is tilted to 16.7°. The coefficient of friction is

  2. 0.29

  4. 0.96

Angle of repose = friction angle, so μ=tan⁡16.7∘=0.30\mu = \tan 16.7^\circ = 0.30μ=tan16.7∘=0.30.

Q2A screw has helix angle 4° and μ=0.1\mu = 0.1μ=0.1 (ϕ=5.7∘\phi = 5.7^\circϕ=5.7∘). The load

  1. stays put: the screw is self-locking
  2. runs down by itself
  3. needs no effort to lift
  4. cannot be lifted

ϕ>α\phi > \alphaϕ>α, so the screw is self-locking; its efficiency is below 50%.

Q3For a new clutch you should calculate the torque capacity using

  3. uniform pressure, because the clutch is new

The clutch will certainly transmit the uniform-wear torque, which is the lower one.
